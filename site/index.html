A forecaster explains the Sun by hand, twice a day. We taught a model to try.

Solar flares and geomagnetic storms threaten power grids, GPS, satellites, and aviation. NOAA's Space Weather Prediction Center forecasts them from raw instrument readings, written out in plain English, every twelve hours, by a person. SpaceWeather-CoT is a new TimeNet dataset pairing that forecaster's own words with the data behind them, and two small models built on it: one that writes a forecast in the forecaster's own voice, and one that calculates the numbers behind it. The result explains itself in a forecaster's own language — and says, in public, exactly where it still gets it wrong.

What feeds the forecast

noaa/spaceweather-cot pairs NOAA's own twice-daily Forecast Discussion — written by a human forecaster, not distilled through another model — with the GOES-16 and OMNI2 instrument data behind it. It's TimeNet's first space-weather dataset, and its first built on genuinely expert-written chain-of-thought rather than an LLM caption of one.

Two models, one job split in two

A forecaster both writes and calculates. We gave each half to a different model, after finding that one model asked to do both does the writing well and the arithmetic badly.

Writes the narrative

Fine-tuned OpenTSLM

Llama-3.2-1B with a LoRA adapter, started from OpenTSLM's ECG-reasoning checkpoint. Reads the five input channels and writes a structurally correct, NOAA-formatted discussion — the voice above. It does not reliably ground specific facts in the data it was given.

Predicts the numbers

Gradient-boosted decision model

Physics features — flare class, flare counts, Bz, solar-wind shocks, Kp — plus persistence from the previous one to two issuances, which turns out to carry most of the signal. The same approach NOAA itself uses to calibrate raw guidance: Model Output Statistics.

Held out: January–April 2025, never seen in training

Two of three clear the 0.5 bar we set for this round; S1 falls short, and we know why. S1 radiation-storm risk is defined by >10 MeV proton flux — a field that exists in OMNI2's schema but is 100% missing in the 2024–2025 files we checked directly against the raw archive. That's an upstream gap, not a connector defect. GOES's own SEISS proton sensor carries the real signal; ingesting it is the clear next step, not attempted here because it ships as per-day files rather than one pinned annual archive.

The fine-tuned language model, asked to write these same three numbers as free text instead, loses to a trivial mean baseline on two of three. Next-token generation has no notion that "34" is closer to "35" than to "99" — which is why the number comes from a separate, calculated model instead.
