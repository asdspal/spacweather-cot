## Pitch (speak this, ~2:00 at a natural pace)

Solar flares and geomagnetic storms threaten power grids, GPS, satellites, and
aviation. The people who forecast them, NOAA's Space Weather Prediction
Center, do it by hand, twice a day, writing a plain-English discussion from
raw instrument data. Grid operators, satellite operators, and airlines all
depend on that judgment call.

We built SpaceWeather-CoT: a new TimeNet dataset that pairs the forecaster's
own words with the GOES X-ray flux and OMNI solar-wind data behind them. Six
hundred fifty-six issuances, March twenty twenty-four through April twenty
twenty-five. It's TimeNet's first space-weather dataset, and the first built
on genuinely expert-written reasoning, not a caption generated by another
model.

We fine-tuned OpenTSLM on it to write forecasts in the forecaster's own
voice, and it learned the structure almost perfectly. But when we asked that
same model to also predict NOAA's numeric probabilities as free text, it lost
to a trivial baseline on two out of three targets. Language models are known
to be poor at numeric regression through next-token generation, and we
measured that failure directly instead of hiding it.

So we split the job the way a real forecasting operation does. The
fine-tuned model writes the narrative. A separate, lightweight decision
model, using physics features and persistence from the previous issuance,
calculates the numbers — the same way NOAA itself calibrates its own
guidance internally.

On months the model never saw during training, that decision model explains
over half the variance in two of NOAA's three forecasts. And we're
transparent about the one target that still falls short, and exactly why:
the proton-flux field that would explain it is missing upstream in the
public data we used, not something we chose to skip.
